# Report Writing & Quality Assurance — Master Skill

## 1. Purpose

Produce reports that answer the reader's question, demonstrate how conclusions
were reached, distinguish evidence from interpretation, communicate uncertainty
honestly, and support appropriate decisions or further investigation.

Optimize for:

1. Accuracy and factual integrity.
2. Relevance to the report's objectives and audience.
3. Appropriate analytical and methodological rigor.
4. Traceability of claims, data, calculations, and sources.
5. Clarity, logical structure, and efficient communication.
6. Reproducibility where the task requires it.
7. Actionable conclusions and recommendations where appropriate.
8. Compliance with the applicable brief, template, standards, and assessment criteria.

Do not equate quality with length, complexity, visual polish, or the number of
sections.

## 2. Scope and operating rules

Use this skill to plan, draft, review, rewrite, validate, or finalize reports.

Before writing, identify:

- The report's purpose and intended audience.
- The decision, question, hypothesis, or problem being addressed.
- The required deliverable and its expected level of detail.
- Applicable instructions, assessment rubrics, templates, and standards.
- Available evidence, data, files, references, and analytical tools.
- Required calculations, methods, visualizations, and validation.
- Length, formatting, citation, confidentiality, and deadline constraints.

### 2.1 Instruction precedence

Apply requirements in this order:

1. Applicable law, safety, ethics, and confidentiality requirements.
2. Explicit assignment, client, organizational, or regulatory requirements.
3. Relevant discipline-specific standards and reporting guidelines.
4. The principles in this skill.
5. Optional stylistic preferences.

Do not silently override a required template or assessment criterion. If
requirements conflict, identify the conflict and resolve it transparently.

### 2.2 Classify requirements

Every instruction should be treated as one of the following:

- **Mandatory:** Required by the brief, applicable standard, or task.
- **Conditional:** Required when the report's purpose or evidence makes it relevant.
- **Optional:** Useful only when it improves understanding or decision-making.

Never add sections, analyses, or visualizations merely to make a report appear
more sophisticated.

## 3. Core reporting principles

### 3.1 Answer the question first

Every report must have a clear purpose and a defensible answer to its central
question.

- Put the principal finding, conclusion, or decision request where the intended
  audience can find it quickly.
- Align objectives, methods, findings, conclusions, and recommendations.
- Remove content that does not serve the report's purpose.
- Distinguish work completed from work proposed or still outstanding.

### 3.2 Maintain evidence integrity

- Never fabricate facts, measurements, sources, quotations, calculations,
  experiments, results, interviews, or validation.
- Never claim that an analysis, test, review, or procedure was completed unless
  there is evidence that it was performed.
- Distinguish observed facts, reported facts, calculations, interpretations,
  assumptions, estimates, and recommendations.
- Preserve the meaning of source material when paraphrasing.
- Flag missing, contradictory, outdated, or unverifiable evidence.
- Do not convert an estimate into a measured result or an association into
  proof of causation.

### 3.3 Maintain logical traceability

The reader should be able to follow this chain:

**Question → Evidence → Method → Analysis → Finding → Conclusion → Action**

Use only the stages that apply to the report. For each material conclusion,
identify the supporting findings and reasoning. For each material finding,
identify the evidence and method used to obtain it.

### 3.4 Match rigor to risk

Increase verification, documentation, and independent review when errors could
affect safety, health, finances, legal obligations, research validity,
regulatory compliance, or important operational decisions.

Do not imply that a report is independently audited, peer-reviewed, certified,
or compliant unless that status is established.

## 4. Adaptive report architecture

Select the report structure according to purpose, audience, discipline, and
the requirements of the task.

### 4.1 Standard professional report

Use this structure when no specific template is supplied and the report's scope
justifies a formal format.

1. Title and document metadata.
2. Executive summary, if appropriate.
3. Introduction, context, and problem statement.
4. Aim, objectives, research questions, or decision criteria.
5. Scope, assumptions, and limitations.
6. Methods, evidence sources, and analytical approach.
7. Results or findings.
8. Discussion and interpretation.
9. Conclusions.
10. Recommendations or next steps, where appropriate.
11. References.
12. Appendices, where necessary.

Adapt, combine, omit, or reorder sections when the task requires it. Academic
research, engineering practicals, incident reviews, and short status reports
may require different conventions.

### 4.2 Executive summary

Include an executive summary when the report is sufficiently substantial or
the audience needs a standalone overview.

It should answer:

- What problem or question was addressed?
- What approach or evidence was used?
- What are the most important findings?
- What do the findings mean?
- What decision or action is required?

Rules:

- Lead with the principal answer, not a list of section headings.
- Include only findings supported by the report.
- Introduce no unsupported facts or new evidence.
- State material uncertainty or limitations.
- Identify the decision or action required when applicable.
- Keep the summary proportionate to the report; use a maximum of approximately
  10% of the main body as a default guide, not an absolute rule.
- Follow any required abstract, word-count, or summary convention.

### 4.3 Introduction and objectives

Establish the context and explain why the report is necessary.

Define the problem, purpose, scope, and relevant background. State the objectives
or questions clearly enough that a reader can determine whether they were
answered.

Avoid an unnecessarily broad background section. Include only context needed
to understand the investigation, analysis, or decision.

### 4.4 Methods and evidence

Describe how information was obtained, selected, processed, and evaluated.

Include relevant details such as:

- Data or source provenance.
- Study design, sampling, inclusion criteria, and exclusions.
- Equipment, software, tools, versions, and configuration.
- Experimental or operational procedures.
- Variables, definitions, units, and measurement conditions.
- Equations, models, statistical methods, and assumptions.
- Validation, calibration, quality control, and verification.
- Analytical time window and data transformations.
- Ethical, privacy, safety, and reproducibility considerations.

Include enough detail for the intended reader to evaluate the method and,
where required, reproduce the work.

Never claim reproducibility merely because a method is described. Reproducibility
also depends on the availability of data, code, settings, dependencies, and
other necessary materials.

### 4.5 Results and findings

Present the evidence before making broader interpretations.

- Organize results around objectives, research questions, or decision criteria.
- Report relevant positive, negative, null, and unexpected findings.
- State sample sizes, denominators, units, time periods, and comparison groups
  where applicable.
- Report missing data, exclusions, failed tests, and material deviations.
- Distinguish primary outcomes from exploratory or secondary findings.
- Use tables and figures when they communicate evidence more effectively.
- Explain the important result in the text instead of merely referring to a
  table or figure.

Do not invent missing results to make the report appear complete. If work
remains outstanding, identify it explicitly.

### 4.6 Discussion and interpretation

Explain what the findings mean in relation to the report's objectives.

- Interpret the evidence using an appropriate analytical framework.
- Compare results with relevant literature, benchmarks, requirements, or prior
  observations where suitable.
- Explain plausible mechanisms or causes without presenting speculation as fact.
- Address contradictory, unexpected, and inconclusive findings.
- Discuss practical implications and alternative explanations.
- Distinguish statistical significance from practical importance.
- Identify how limitations affect the interpretation.

Do not repeat the results section without adding interpretation.

### 4.7 Conclusions

Provide a concise, evidence-based answer to the report's objectives.

- Address each material objective or research question.
- Base conclusions on findings already presented.
- State what the evidence establishes, suggests, or leaves unresolved.
- Avoid introducing new evidence, analyses, or references.
- Do not overstate causality, certainty, generalizability, or compliance.
- Do not claim that a problem has been solved unless the evidence supports it.

A conclusion should synthesize existing evidence, not introduce a new argument
that the report has not developed.

### 4.8 Recommendations and action plans

Include recommendations when the task requires a decision, improvement, or
follow-up action.

For each material recommendation, specify as appropriate:

- **Action:** What should be done?
- **Rationale:** Which finding justifies the action?
- **Owner:** Who is responsible?
- **Priority:** How urgent or important is it?
- **Timing:** When should it begin or be completed?
- **Resources:** What budget, equipment, personnel, or tools are needed?
- **Dependencies:** What must happen first?
- **Success measure:** How will completion or effectiveness be evaluated?
- **Risk:** What could prevent success, and how can it be mitigated?

Do not invent named owners, deadlines, budgets, or commitments. Use role-based
owners, proposed dates, or explicit placeholders when details are unknown.

Distinguish mandatory corrective actions from optional improvements.

### 4.9 References and appendices

References must allow readers to identify and verify the cited sources.

Appendices may contain supporting calculations, extended tables, instruments,
detailed procedures, supplementary figures, code, configuration files, or
additional evidence.

Keep information necessary to understand the main findings in the body.
Appendices should support the report, not conceal its essential reasoning.

## 5. Report archetypes

Choose one primary archetype and combine others when necessary.

| Archetype | Primary emphasis | Typical additions |
|---|---|---|
| Academic or research | Research question, methods, evidence, interpretation | Literature review, hypotheses, statistical analysis, limitations |
| Engineering or laboratory | Theory, procedure, measured results, verification | Equations, uncertainty, equipment, calculations, error analysis |
| Data or forecasting | Data quality, analytical validity, model performance | Missingness, baselines, validation splits, residual diagnostics, metrics |
| Technical or system design | Requirements, architecture, constraints, trade-offs | Interfaces, diagrams, failure modes, testing, deployment |
| Executive or business | Decision, implications, value, risks | Options, costs, benefits, assumptions, decision request |
| Status or progress | Delivery, changes, blockers, decisions | Milestones, status indicators, owners, next-period actions |
| Incident or post-mortem | Impact, timeline, causes, prevention | Detection, response, contributing factors, corrective actions |
| Audit or compliance | Criteria, evidence, control effectiveness | Sampling, findings grades, exceptions, remediation |
| Evaluation or options | Comparative merit and uncertainty | Decision criteria, weighted matrix, sensitivity analysis |
| Policy or strategy | Problem, alternatives, consequences | Stakeholder impacts, implementation, monitoring |
| Review or due diligence | Completeness, credibility, material risks | Evidence gaps, verification status, risk register |

State the selected archetype when it materially affects the structure or
methodology and is not otherwise obvious.

Follow discipline-specific reporting guidance and organizational requirements
when applicable.

## 6. Structure and formatting conventions

### 6.1 Headings and organization

- Use a logical hierarchy, normally no deeper than three heading levels.
- Make headings informative and specific.
- Prefer descriptive headings such as "Network congestion increased after
  the routing change" over generic headings such as "Findings."
- Ensure headings reflect the content that follows.
- Use numbering when it improves navigation or is required by the template.
- Maintain a clear flow from context through evidence and interpretation to
  conclusions and actions.

### 6.2 Paragraphs and lists

- Give each paragraph one principal purpose.
- Lead with the main point, followed by evidence and explanation.
- Use lists for parallel items, procedures, criteria, and actions.
- Use prose for reasoning that requires continuity.
- Break up dense paragraphs when doing so improves comprehension.
- Avoid fragments unless the document format supports them.

A paragraph of three to six sentences is a useful starting point, not a rigid
rule.

### 6.3 Tables and figures

- Number tables and figures consistently when formal numbering is required.
- Provide descriptive titles or captions.
- Include units in headings, labels, or captions.
- Identify sources and explain material abbreviations.
- Refer to each numbered table or figure in the text before or near its
  appearance.
- Explain what the evidence shows and why it matters.
- Use appropriate scales, readable labels, and clearly identified comparison
  groups.
- Avoid redundant charts that add no information beyond an existing table.
- Never manipulate visual scales or omit material context to exaggerate a result.

For complex quantitative work, document relevant data-processing and plotting
choices so that figures can be checked or regenerated.

### 6.4 Numbers, dates, and units

- Use consistent units, precision, significant figures, and decimal places.
- Preserve the precision justified by the measurements and method.
- Use unambiguous dates, such as 19 October 2026 or 2026-10-19.
- Identify currency, reporting period, denominator, and comparison basis.
- Define percentages clearly and distinguish percentage-point changes from
  relative percentage changes.
- Show formulas and intermediate calculations when necessary for verification.
- Reconcile subtotals, totals, and reported metrics.
- Apply relevant discipline-specific notation and unit conventions.

### 6.5 Acronyms and terminology

- Expand an acronym at first use unless it is universally understood by the
  intended audience.
- Use terms consistently throughout the report.
- Provide a glossary when technical terminology or acronym volume warrants it.
- Define metrics and indicators before interpreting them.
- Explain specialist concepts in language appropriate to the reader.

### 6.6 Presentation and accessibility

- Use consistent typography, spacing, numbering, and table formatting.
- Use bold sparingly for important terms and decisions.
- Avoid excessive capitalization, decorative formatting, and unnecessary jargon.
- Ensure figures, tables, and diagrams remain understandable when viewed or
  printed at their intended size.
- Provide meaningful captions and alternative text where the output format
  supports accessibility features.

## 7. Language and tone

Use professional, direct, precise, neutral language unless the intended audience
requires a different style.

- Prefer active voice when the actor is known and relevant.
- Use passive voice when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or conventionally
  omitted in the discipline.
- Remove filler, repetition, vague intensifiers, and unsupported superlatives.
- Prefer concrete evidence over general statements.
- Avoid "clearly," "obviously," "various," and "etc." when precise wording is
  available.
- Use "significant" carefully, distinguishing statistical significance from
  practical importance.
- Keep tense consistent with the type of statement and reporting convention.
- Do not sacrifice technical precision for simplicity.

### 7.1 Calibrate confidence

| Evidence status | Appropriate phrasing |
|---|---|
| Directly established by reliable evidence | "The measurements show..." |
| Supported but not conclusively established | "The evidence suggests..." |
| Plausible but unverified | "This may indicate..." |
| Dependent on an assumption | "Assuming X, the result is..." |
| Not adequately investigated | "This was not assessed." |
| Checked but unresolved | "The available evidence does not establish..." |
| Contradicted by available evidence | State the contradiction explicitly |

Choose wording according to the actual evidence. Do not hedge established facts
unnecessarily or present uncertain interpretations as proven.

## 8. Evidence, sourcing, and integrity

### 8.1 Source verification

- Attribute externally sourced, non-obvious factual claims to appropriate sources.
- Prefer original research, primary records, official documentation, standards,
  and authoritative datasets when suitable.
- Use secondary sources to provide context or synthesis where appropriate.
- Verify the author or issuing organization, title, date, relevance, and source
  accessibility when possible.
- Use the required citation style consistently.
- Ensure every in-text citation has a corresponding reference and vice versa,
  where the chosen style requires this.
- Confirm that cited sources actually support the associated claims.
- Never invent references, page numbers, identifiers, quotations, or URLs.

If a source cannot be verified, mark the reference as unverified or replace it
with a suitable verified source.

### 8.2 Claim-to-evidence traceability

For material claims, establish:

1. What exactly is being claimed?
2. What evidence supports it?
3. Where did the evidence originate?
4. What method or reasoning connects the evidence to the claim?
5. What uncertainty, limitation, or alternative explanation applies?

For high-stakes or complex reports, maintain an evidence matrix linking
objectives, claims, sources, analyses, findings, and conclusions.

### 8.3 Source quality and conflicts

- Assess relevance, authority, recency, independence, and methodological quality.
- Identify conflicts of interest or sponsor influence when material.
- Report disagreements between sources rather than silently selecting the
  convenient version.
- Explain why one source or interpretation is preferred when the distinction
  matters.
- Distinguish a source's claim from an independently verified fact.

### 8.4 AI and automated tools

When AI or automated tools materially contribute to analysis or content,
follow applicable disclosure requirements.

- Verify generated factual claims, equations, calculations, references, and code.
- Do not treat generated output as independent evidence.
- Identify which calculations, analyses, or checks were actually performed.
- Preserve human accountability for the final report.
- Do not submit confidential, personal, or restricted information to tools
  without appropriate authorization and safeguards.

## 9. Quantitative and technical validation

Apply this section whenever the report contains numerical analysis, experiments,
models, forecasts, engineering calculations, or technical claims.

### 9.1 Data quality

Inspect relevant data for:

- Missing values and missing intervals.
- Duplicate or invalid records.
- Incorrect data types, units, timestamps, and labels.
- Outliers and implausible measurements.
- Sampling inconsistencies and selection bias.
- Data leakage and inappropriate exclusions.
- Changes in collection methods or definitions.
- Representativeness and coverage limitations.

Document material cleaning decisions and their effects on the results.

Do not silently remove inconvenient observations or modify data solely to
produce a preferred outcome.

### 9.2 Calculations and statistical analysis

- Define variables, parameters, and metrics.
- Select methods appropriate to the research question and data structure.
- Verify formulas, units, assumptions, and calculation steps.
- Distinguish descriptive statistics from inferential conclusions.
- Report uncertainty, confidence intervals, or error bounds when appropriate.
- Check statistical assumptions and diagnostic results.
- Distinguish correlation, association, prediction, and causation.
- Report effect sizes and practical implications when relevant.
- Address multiple testing, sample size, and model selection where applicable.

Do not report statistical tests, confidence intervals, or significance levels
that have not actually been calculated.

### 9.3 Experiments and engineering work

When relevant:

- State the theoretical or engineering basis.
- Identify equipment, configuration, initial conditions, and test procedures.
- Distinguish theoretical predictions from measured observations.
- Include units and uncertainty or tolerance estimates.
- Compare measurements with expected values, specifications, or benchmarks.
- Calculate error using an explicitly defined reference.
- Investigate deviations rather than hiding them.
- Identify whether results satisfy the specified acceptance criteria.

### 9.4 Forecasting and predictive models

When relevant:

- Define the prediction target and forecast horizon.
- Establish a defensible baseline.
- Use validation procedures appropriate to the data and intended deployment.
- Prevent information leakage.
- Select metrics appropriate to the prediction problem.
- Compare model performance against the baseline.
- Inspect residuals and relevant diagnostic tests.
- Evaluate stability, uncertainty, and limitations.
- State whether performance was measured on training, validation, or test data.

A model is not justified merely because it produces predictions. Demonstrate
whether it adds value for the intended use.

### 9.5 Reproducibility

For analyses that need to be reproduced, preserve or document:

- Source data and provenance.
- Data-cleaning and transformation steps.
- Equations, model specifications, and parameter choices.
- Code, scripts, and dependencies where applicable.
- Software and relevant version information.
- Random seeds when relevant.
- Validation procedures and output-generation steps.
- Expected outputs and verification checks.

Do not expose restricted data or secrets in reproducibility materials.

## 10. Handling uncertainty, limitations, and disagreement

Include an explicit assumptions and limitations section when the report's scope,
method, evidence, or intended use warrants it.

Distinguish:

- **Known:** Supported by the available evidence.
- **Assumed:** Accepted provisionally for analysis.
- **Estimated:** Derived using an estimation method.
- **Unverified:** Not independently checked or adequately substantiated.
- **Unknown:** Not established by the available evidence.
- **Not assessed:** Outside the work actually performed.
- **Conflicting:** Supported by materially inconsistent evidence.

For each material limitation, explain its likely effect on the findings,
conclusions, or recommended action.

When alternatives are plausible, compare them and explain which evidence would
help distinguish between them.

Use sensitivity analysis or scenarios when reasonable changes in assumptions
could materially change the result.

Do not manufacture numerical confidence levels or probability estimates
without an appropriate basis.

## 11. Risk, ethics, and compliance

Where applicable:

- Identify safety, privacy, security, legal, ethical, operational, and financial
  risks.
- Distinguish inherent risk from residual risk after controls.
- State the criteria used to grade findings or assign risk levels.
- Prioritize risks using an appropriate and consistently applied method.
- Identify mitigations, owners, dependencies, and monitoring requirements.
- Protect confidential information and remove unnecessary personal data.
- Distinguish recommendations from binding legal or regulatory requirements.
- Cite the relevant standard, policy, or regulation when making a compliance
  claim.

Never declare a system, organization, or process compliant solely because the
report did not identify a violation.

## 12. Pre-delivery quality assurance

Perform a final review proportionate to the report's complexity and risk.

### 12.1 Content and alignment

- [ ] The report answers the stated question or explains why it cannot yet do so.
- [ ] Objectives and scope are explicit.
- [ ] Every material objective is addressed in the findings or conclusions.
- [ ] Important claims are supported by evidence or transparent reasoning.
- [ ] Conclusions follow from the presented findings.
- [ ] Recommendations are justified and actionable where required.
- [ ] Outstanding work and unresolved questions are identified.
- [ ] No essential information is buried in an appendix.

### 12.2 Accuracy and analytical integrity

- [ ] Figures, calculations, units, totals, and denominators reconcile.
- [ ] Data transformations and exclusions are documented where relevant.
- [ ] Methods are suitable for the question and evidence.
- [ ] Assumptions and limitations are stated.
- [ ] Uncertainty and alternative explanations are handled appropriately.
- [ ] Baselines, validation, and diagnostics are included when required.
- [ ] No analysis or validation is claimed without evidence of completion.

### 12.3 References and traceability

- [ ] Material externally sourced claims have appropriate attribution.
- [ ] References are real, relevant, and verifiable to the extent possible.
- [ ] Citations support the claims attached to them.
- [ ] In-text citations and the reference list are consistent.
- [ ] Data provenance and analytical methods are documented as required.
- [ ] AI-assisted content and automated outputs have been checked.

### 12.4 Structure and language

- [ ] The structure fits the report archetype and required template.
- [ ] The executive summary answers the reader's question when included.
- [ ] Headings accurately describe their content.
- [ ] Terminology, tense, notation, and formatting are consistent.
- [ ] Acronyms and technical metrics are defined appropriately.
- [ ] Repetition, filler, vague claims, and unnecessary sections are removed.
- [ ] The report is understandable to its intended audience.

### 12.5 Presentation and delivery

- [ ] Tables and figures are numbered, captioned, and referenced as required.
- [ ] Labels, scales, units, and sources are clear.
- [ ] Metadata is complete where required.
- [ ] The file opens correctly and uses the requested format.
- [ ] Accessibility, confidentiality, and submission requirements are met.
- [ ] The final document has been checked for missing sections and formatting errors.

## 13. Severity-based defect handling

Classify identified problems according to their effect on the report.

| Severity | Description | Required response |
|---|---|---|
| Critical | Fabrication, invalid central conclusion, serious safety issue, or material integrity failure | Stop delivery until resolved or the report is explicitly reframed |
| Major | Material analytical error, unsupported central claim, missing required analysis, or noncompliance with a critical requirement | Correct before final delivery or explicitly label the deliverable as incomplete |
| Moderate | Weak explanation, incomplete supporting evidence, inconsistent presentation, or a noncritical omission | Correct where practical and document unresolved limitations |
| Minor | Typographical, stylistic, or low-impact formatting issue | Correct during final editing |

Do not use a polished presentation to disguise a critical or major defect.

### 13.1 Final delivery status

Assign a status that accurately describes the document:

- **Final:** Required work is complete and material checks have passed.
- **Final with disclosed limitations:** The deliverable is complete for its stated
  purpose, with known limitations that do not invalidate that purpose.
- **Draft:** Material review, analysis, or approval remains outstanding.
- **Incomplete:** Required work is missing or the central question cannot yet be
  answered adequately.

A status label does not replace disclosure of material limitations.

## 14. Anti-patterns

Avoid:

- Burying the principal answer beneath unnecessary background.
- Summarizing section headings instead of communicating findings.
- Presenting raw data without interpretation.
- Making recommendations without rationale or an actionable next step.
- Reporting invented, unverified, or irreproducible results as facts.
- Treating estimates, assumptions, and observations as interchangeable.
- Claiming causality from evidence that only supports association.
- Overstating statistical or practical significance.
- Hiding missing data, failed tests, contradictory findings, or inconvenient results.
- Using a model without an appropriate baseline or validation strategy.
- Including charts that add no meaningful information.
- Using length, jargon, or excessive formatting as a substitute for substance.
- Treating a checklist as proof that the analysis itself is correct.
- Forcing every report into the same structure.
- Claiming independent verification, certification, or compliance without support.

## 15. Delivery format

Unless the task or template specifies otherwise, use Markdown for text-based
reports.

Include only the components that the report needs:

1. Title and metadata.
2. Executive summary or abstract, where appropriate.
3. Numbered main sections.
4. Tables and figures where they add value.
5. Conclusions.
6. Recommendations or next steps, where appropriate.
7. References when sources are used or required.
8. Appendices when supplementary material is necessary.

Metadata may include title, author, date, version, status, intended audience,
classification, and reporting period.

Use the requested format when a report must be delivered as DOCX, PDF, XLSX,
PPTX, or another specified artifact. Check the exported document rather than
assuming that correct source text guarantees correct final formatting.

### 15.1 Required-template compliance

When an assignment, organization, journal, client, or regulator supplies a
template:

- Preserve required headings, order, numbering, and formatting.
- Map the principles in this skill to the supplied structure.
- Identify missing information without fabricating it.
- Explain any unavoidable deviation when appropriate.
- Do not add optional sections that conflict with the template.

## 16. Standard execution workflow

Follow these stages in order, adapting the effort to the task.

### Stage 1 — Understand

Identify the purpose, audience, requirements, scope, and deliverable.

### Stage 2 — Inspect

Review available documents, datasets, references, existing drafts, and
instructions. Identify evidence gaps before drafting.

### Stage 3 — Plan

Choose the report archetype, structure, methods, validation requirements,
and appropriate level of detail.

### Stage 4 — Analyze

Perform the required calculations, research, comparisons, experiments,
or technical checks. Record assumptions and unresolved issues.

### Stage 5 — Draft

Write findings and analysis in a logical sequence. Ensure every section serves
the report's objectives.

### Stage 6 — Verify

Check evidence, calculations, references, interpretations, conclusions,
recommendations, and applicable requirements.

### Stage 7 — Edit

Improve clarity, structure, precision, consistency, accessibility, and
presentation without changing the underlying evidence.

### Stage 8 — Release

Apply the severity checks, assign the appropriate delivery status, and
deliver the requested artifact with any necessary limitations disclosed.

Do not claim completion of a stage that was not performed.

## 17. Definition of success

A successful report:

- Answers the intended question as directly as the evidence allows.
- Meets the applicable brief and reporting requirements.
- Uses appropriate methods and verifiable evidence.
- Makes its reasoning and important calculations traceable.
- Communicates uncertainty and limitations honestly.
- Separates evidence, interpretation, conclusions, and proposed actions.
- Provides useful recommendations when the task calls for them.
- Can be understood by its intended audience.
- Contains no known unresolved critical defects.
- Is delivered in the requested format with an accurate status.

**Final rule:** Be rigorous where correctness matters, concise where detail adds
no value, explicit where uncertainty remains, and transparent about what was
and was not established.
